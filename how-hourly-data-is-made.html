How hourly weather data is made

Most hourly forecasts are not hourly. Past the first few days the gaps are filled in, and for rain that matters more than it sounds.

Background: weather models

Weather models predict by simulating physics. A few times a day, supercomputers ingest observations from satellites, balloons, ground stations, aircraft and ships, then calculate how air, heat and moisture move forward in time, step by step.

+

The atmosphere is split into a grid of cells stacked in layers from the ground (green) to the upper atmosphere (gold). Smaller cells and more layers give better detail but cost exponentially more computation, so models trade resolution for range at longer lead times.

What one request returns

A single request to Open-Meteo, the free weather API used here, returns hourly values for the next 16 days. Three of those fields carry most of a rain forecast:

What Open-Meteo is, and what a request means

A request is a URL

Paste it into a browser and a server sends back numbers as plain text rather than a web page. No account or key needed. The exact URL is at the bottom under Verify it.

Open-Meteo is not a forecaster

It is an open-data service that collects forecasts national weather agencies already publish — ECMWF in Europe, NOAA in the United States, DWD in Germany, and others — converts them into one consistent format, and serves them free of charge.

This distinction matters. The cadence limits below are from the national models. The gap-filling that extends them to hourly is Open-Meteo's, and it's documented.

Which model answers a request

The request carries models=best_match, meaning Open-Meteo picks whichever model it judges best for that location. European and US requests are answered by different agencies, which is why the boundary measured in the next section varies by location.

The next full hour, live

Eleven more arrive in the same response: apparent temperature, humidity, cloud cover, wind speed, direction and gusts, UV index, snowfall and a weather code.

All of them arrive as one value per hour, out to day 16. But a model doesn't compute each one separately. What's here is the API padded to hourly, and only some of those hours are real.

Hourly data runs out around day four

Computation is expensive, so models step down their cadence as they reach further. Start at hourly, drop to every 3 hours, then every 6.

Cadence also varies by run: HRRR reaches 48 hours on its main cycles and 18 on the others, so a hard-coded table goes stale within hours.

After that, the hours are filled in

A spline is fit through the computed points, then the missing hours are interpolated from it. Hiding all but every 3rd hour and refitting the curve shows the difference: if the hidden hours match when revealed, they were computed; if they don't, they were filled in from their neighbors.

How the test works

A green block near the start does not reconstruct, because those hours are real. An orange block near the end reconstructs almost exactly. Switching to straight lines makes the rebuild miss everywhere, which is what identifies the interpolation as cubic.

Reading the strip

computed — a model produced every hour here 3-hourly — 1 hour in 3 computed, the other 2 filled in 6-hourly — 1 hour in 6 computed, the other 5 filled in can't tell — too flat to leave a signal, or inside the transition band where neither rebuild fits

The formula

A Catmull-Rom spline: a curve through two computed points p1 and p2, with its slope at each end set by the neighbours p0 and p3.

value(t) = a·t³ + b·t² + c·t + d where t runs 0→1 between p1 and p2 a = ½(−p0 + 3p1 − 3p2 + p3) b = ½(2p0 − 5p1 + 4p2 − p3) c = ½(−p0 + p2) d = p1

Temperatures arrive rounded to 0.1°. Where hours are derived the rebuild misses by 0.06–0.08°; where they were computed it misses by 0.2°–0.7°. The test reads that separation.

Rainfall amount is not interpolated at all

Rainfall is a total over a period rather than a rate at an instant, so there is nothing to interpolate. Open-Meteo divides the period total evenly and repeats the same value, which states the model's real resolution directly.

04Measured

Chance of rain was never hourly

Rainfall amount and probability come from the same forecast, so they should have the same resolution. They don't.

Measured live, right now

The amount arrives in blocks. The probability changes every hour, and no source exists for that hour-to-hour structure, because the model produced one number for the whole block.

Why window probabilities can't split to hourly

Temperature is defined at any point in time, so interpolating it only costs precision. A window probability is not: 60% over 3 hours is fundamentally different from 60% in one hour.

Rain is correlated hour to hour, so the true hourly chance sits between the independent lower bound and the window value. Assigning the window to each hour claims the upper bound every time.

Where the hourly probability comes from

Of 63 models in Open-Meteo, only one stores a chance of rain: NOAA's National Blend, and only for the continental US. No global model does.

Everywhere else it's derived from an ensemble, and every global ensemble is 3-hourly or coarser. ECMWF's ensemble—which covers most places—is 3-hourly from the start. So for most locations, hourly chance of rain is filled in, even on day one.

measured, mechanism inferred The rebuild test can't identify the smoothing method for probability like it does for temperature, because percentages are integers and too blunt a signal. The smoothing is visible; the method is not.

The rebuild test on probability

Shown for completeness, but with limits: a successful rebuild indicates smoothing, not proof of it. Dry forecasts report "can't tell" because flat values fit any cadence.

The exception: the United States

Over the continental US, the National Blend is stored unsmoothed, held flat across windows. The boundaries jump in the data directly. This is what all raw ensemble data looks like—elsewhere it's masked by smoothing.
